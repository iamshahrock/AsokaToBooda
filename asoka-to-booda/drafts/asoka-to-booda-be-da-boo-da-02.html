CHAPTER 1 — 2011: RA.ONE AND THE INVENTION OF A DIGITAL KINGDOM

The year a Bollywood film tried to become an operating system

Before artificial intelligence became a consumer obsession, before every brand had an AI strategy and before generative content threatened to turn every fan into a production studio, Shah Rukh Khan attempted something remarkably ambitious.

He tried to make a Bollywood film live beyond its theatrical release.

Ra.One was not simply a superhero movie with a marketing campaign attached. Its public-facing ecosystem included character merchandising, gaming, technology partnerships, a dedicated website, a graphic novel and an extensive promotional programme. The campaign treated G.ONE as an asset that could exist across multiple formats, not merely as a character whose life ended with the credits.

The ambition was unmistakable.

The character could be played with, collected, discussed, reproduced and experienced through different media. The audience could encounter the fictional world outside the cinema.

This was the beginning of a different commercial proposition for Bollywood.

A conventional film campaign asks:

How do we get people to buy tickets?

The Ra.One experiment asked a more expansive question:

How do we make people want to live inside the world of the film?

That difference is the foundation of the entire thesis.

A film is a time-bound product. An entertainment universe can become a continuing relationship.

A film has a release window. An intellectual property can generate games, products, stories, experiences and communities for years.

And a fan is no longer merely an audience member. A fan can become a participant in the commercial life of the fictional world.

There were obvious challenges. Building an entertainment universe requires more than an idea. It requires technology, capital, rights management, distribution, continuity and a long-term commitment to developing the property.

But Ra.One placed those challenges directly in front of the Indian film industry.

It demonstrated that a Bollywood superstar could use his commercial power to attempt something structurally different from a conventional film launch.

And it left behind a question that remains relevant in 2026:

Why did the promise of the Bollywood digital universe never become the default operating model of the industry?
