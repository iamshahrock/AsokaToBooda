Chapter 17 · Five Hypotheses

Why did the virtual train never become a permanent railway? Why did the superhero never become a continuing franchise? Why did the participatory campaign remain a celebrated exception instead of an industry model? There are at least five plausible explanations. None of them is a verified account of anyone’s private decisions. (Analysis)

  1. The economics of experimentation. Games, merchandise and permanent platforms cost money beyond a film’s promotional budget, and the returns arrive slowly, if at all. Repeating the experiment film after film may simply not have paid.
  2. The platform trap. As audiences moved to social platforms and apps, a campaign could reach millions without building any technology of its own. That brought scale and speed, and handed the relationship with the audience to someone else’s platform.
  3. The franchise gap. A character, a game or a line of toys earns over years only when rights, investment, distribution and creative continuity line up. A brilliant launch does not make a franchise by itself.
  4. The release-cycle logic. Film companies are organised around release dates, opening weekends and short promotional windows. A fan ecosystem that runs all year needs a different organisation, with year-round owners and operators.
  5. The human dimension. Creative priorities, working relationships, leadership decisions and who is given responsibility all decide whether an experiment continues. Establishing what happened here would take the internal record and the testimony of the people involved.

(Analysis) The five are not exclusive; all of them may have played a part. The public record does not say which, if any, was decisive. That is the research gap, and this book leaves it open rather than filling it with a villain. (Method note) What makes the gap intriguing is that the technology was not standing still. In these same years the smartphone, cheap data and social video arrived in India at a scale the 2000 website could only have dreamed of. (Analysis)
