Chapter 42 · The Third Wheel

(Proposal) The playful card. A campaign in which Shah Rukh Khan is the charming third wheel of a fan couple’s story: the friend who turns up on the date, the celebrity who becomes part of the fan’s personal life rather than a face on a poster. For thirty years he has been the country’s idea of a lover. This idea makes him its best friend instead, and turns fandom into a relationship rather than a transaction. (Proposal; the author’s concept) Under #FreeMyFace, it would be made the honest way: with the fans’ own faces, and his only with his consent. (Analysis)

How the card would play. At its centre is the author’s Third Wheel Manifesto: nobody gets left out of love. A couple may fall in love and a third friend may love them both, and nobody has to become the villain, the loser or the forgotten one. Friendship is not a consolation prize. (Proposal; the author’s concept)

As a game it becomes the KING Third-Wheel Challenge. A fan picks a character from one of his films, adds a moment from their own life, and meets a dilemma: your best friend has fallen in love with someone you also love; the couple wants you in their lives, and you are hurting. Then the fan chooses: confess and let everyone decide honestly; step back and protect the friendship; or say you want to stay an important friend, not a rival. Each answer opens a different next scene, and none has to end with someone left behind. (Proposal; the author’s game design)

The love triangles of Hindi cinema have usually needed a loser: the friend who sacrifices, the rival who leaves. This card asks the audience to find a way forward for all three. (Analysis)

The triangles he has already played. The card does not start from nothing. Hindi cinema’s best-known triangle before him, Raj Kapoor’s Sangam (1964), was a story of three childhood friends, Sundar, Radha and Gopal; it ends with Gopal, the friend who loved in silence, dying by his own hand with Sundar’s pistol, and it was the highest-grossing Hindi film of its year. (Fact. Read down the endings and the pattern is plain: in every one of these films the third person dies, is killed, or steps aside. The audiences who paid for those endings are the same audiences the Third Wheel card asks to write a fourth kind, in which the third person stays in the story. (Analysis) In 2003 he played Aman, the third who gives the girl away; the card asks the fan to play Aman and keep him alive and loved. (Proposal; the author’s concept)
