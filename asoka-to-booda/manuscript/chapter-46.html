The road to O TO X. The game is planned in three stages, not built all at once. (Proposal)

Stage What it adds Its test
Phase 1 · Playable MVP Player accounts and personal libraries; holiday tiles and checked 100-word ideas; shared 60-second rounds; server-verified scoring and a live leaderboard; personal media private by default Do strangers enjoy creating and competing in the same round?
Phase 2 · Creative platform MY SUM; AI-assisted creation; missions generated from original ideas; creator profiles, credits and reusable assets; themed community tournaments Does the game stay interesting beyond the KING theme?
Phase 3 · Global universe Player-designed worlds and game modes; licensed entertainment partnerships; creator revenue-sharing and optional licensing; global championships and community seasons Can it grow through the players’ imagination rather than one star or one film?

(Proposal; the author’s game plan) A first multiplayer scaffold, a browser front end with a Node.js game server for rooms, matchmaking, the clock and scoring, was drafted in the author’s design sessions. It keeps games in memory, so rooms vanish when the server restarts; accounts, a database, reconnection, secure image storage and a WebSocket host come next. It is not yet online. The law that now frames the board. The Government notified the rules under the 2025 Online Gaming Act on 22 April 2026, in force from 1 May 2026. Most online social games need neither a determination nor a registration; registration applies only where the Government notifies it, weighing risk to users including children, scale, money flows and the provider’s home country. E-sports must register. Providers of social games and e-sports must keep a grievance channel, with appeal to a new Online Gaming Authority of India within 30 days. (Fact. PTI via DT Next, 22 Apr 2026) A free fan dashboard that pays only in points sits on the light side of that line; the duty most likely to reach it is a published way for players to complain and be answered. (Analysis; whether a fan site counts as a “provider” under the rules is not settled here)

The market the board plays in. Lumikai, a games investor, counted India’s video-game market at US$1.5 billion in calendar 2025, excluding real-money games, up 17%; the player base fell from 609 million to 555 million after the money-game ban, while one in four players still paid. A casual player spent about US$3 on average against US$15 for a mid-core player, and the firm projects US$3.2 billion by 2030. (Fact. Lumikai State of India Interactive Media Report 2025, via Outlook Respawn, 23 Mar 2026) The FICCI-EY count quoted in Chapter 33 reads the same year as a 17% fall; Lumikai leaves money games out, and the EY release does not print its definition, so both figures stand side by side here. (Method note) At three dollars a casual payer, a fan board would not pay its way through in-game purchases; its money has to come from merchandise, sponsors and the films themselves, while play stays free.
